# Writing Skill — Publication-Quality Analytical Prose

## When to Use

Apply to all narrative text: chart descriptions, executive summaries, report sections, key findings, methodology notes, and data commentary. The bar is NYT/Economist/WSJ analytical writing — point-first, precise, and free of filler.

## Foundation: Orwell's Six Rules

Every rule in this skill derives from these principles (from "Politics and the English Language," 1946):

1. Never use a metaphor, simile, or other figure of speech you are used to seeing in print.
2. Never use a long word where a short one will do.
3. If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out.
4. Never use the passive where you can use the active.
5. Never use a foreign phrase, scientific word, or jargon term if you can think of an everyday equivalent.
6. Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous.

**Zinsser's additions** (from "On Writing Well"):
- Rewriting is the essence of writing. The first draft is never the final product.
- Clutter is the disease of writing. Every word that serves no function should go.
- Writing improves in direct ratio to the number of things you can keep out of it.

## Structure — Point-First Architecture

Lead with the conclusion. Readers decide in the first sentence whether to keep reading.

### BLUF (Bottom Line Up Front)

State the finding before explaining how you got there. The reader should know the answer before encountering the evidence.

**Weak:** "After examining population data from 2010 to 2024 across all ten provinces, applying seasonal adjustments and controlling for interprovincial migration, we found that Alberta grew fastest."
**Strong:** "Alberta grew faster than any other province from 2010 to 2024 — its population expanded 32%, double the national average."

### The Nut Graf

Within two paragraphs, answer two questions: what is this about, and why does it matter? If the reader stops after paragraph two, they should still walk away informed.

**Missing nut graf:** "The dataset contains 15 years of crime statistics across 200 municipalities. We cleaned the data, normalized for population, and grouped by region. The following sections present our findings on trends, severity, and geographic distribution."
**With nut graf:** "Crime in British Columbia has fallen 22% since 2010 — but the decline is concentrated in cities. Rural communities saw no improvement, and in some cases got worse. This report maps where the gains went, who was left behind, and what drove the divergence."

### Inverted Pyramid at Paragraph Level

Every paragraph follows: **claim → evidence → implication**.

- **Claim:** The one-sentence point this paragraph exists to make.
- **Evidence:** The data, comparison, or source that supports the claim.
- **Implication:** Why this matters — the "so what."

**Inverted (weak):** "Statistics Canada collects crime data annually from police services across the country. The 2024 release covers 200+ jurisdictions. After adjusting for population, the data reveals that BC's crime rate dropped to its lowest point in two decades."
**Pyramid (strong):** "BC's crime rate hit a two-decade low in 2024. *[claim]* The Crime Severity Index fell to 78.2, down from 101.4 in 2003, based on Statistics Canada's annual police-reported data. *[evidence]* The milestone suggests that long-term policy investments are producing measurable returns — but only in urban centres. *[implication]*"

### Forward Momentum

End paragraphs by opening the next question, not by summarizing what was just said. The last sentence should pull the reader forward *when the analysis continues*. If the "so what" is genuinely conclusive (the honest answer is backward-looking), state it and move on — manufactured forward momentum is worse than an honest conclusion.

**Weak ending:** "This confirms that crime severity declined in 2023."
**Strong ending:** "Crime severity declined in 2023 — but not uniformly, and the exceptions reveal where resources fell short."

## Sentences — Mechanics of Clarity

### Active Voice as Default

Passive voice hides the actor and drains energy. Use it only when the actor is genuinely unknown or irrelevant.

**Passive:** "A 15% increase in funding was approved by the council."
**Active:** "The council approved a 15% funding increase."

Target: fewer than 10% of sentences in passive voice (AI default hovers near 28%).

### Front-Load Meaning

Put the key concept at the start of the sentence. Readers scan left-to-right; burying the point after a subordinate clause wastes their attention.

**Buried:** "Despite facing headwinds from supply chain disruptions and rising input costs throughout the fiscal year, the company posted record revenue."
**Front-loaded:** "The company posted record revenue despite supply chain disruptions and rising input costs."

### Vary Sentence Length

Aim for roughly one-third short, one-third medium, one-third long:

- **Short (under 10 words):** Punchy. Creates emphasis. Breaks rhythm.
- **Medium (12–24 words):** Carries most analytical prose. Balances clarity with nuance.
- **Long (25+ words):** Handles complex comparisons, conditional logic, multi-part evidence.

**Monotone (all medium):** "Crime rates declined by 22% over the decade. Property crime drove most of the improvement. Violent crime remained largely unchanged during this period. The trend held across most urban centres."
**Varied rhythm:** "Crime fell 22% over the decade. Property crime drove the drop — auto theft and break-and-enter together accounted for four-fifths of the reduction, a concentration that surprised even veteran analysts. Violent crime barely moved."

### The 1-2 Punch

Follow a complex sentence with a short declarative for impact.

"Between 2018 and 2023, property crime rates fell across every major metropolitan area in British Columbia, driven by a combination of targeted policing, community intervention programs, and improved economic conditions in the Lower Mainland. **Violent crime did not follow.**"

### Kill Nominalizations

Nominalizations bury action inside nouns. Reverse them.

| Nominalization | Direct verb |
|---|---|
| made an assessment of | assessed |
| conducted an investigation into | investigated |
| reached a conclusion that | concluded |
| experienced an increase | increased |
| showed improvement in | improved |

## Word Choice — Precision Over Decoration

### Orwell's Rule

Never use a long word where a short one will do. Never use a foreign phrase, scientific word, or jargon term if an everyday equivalent exists.

| Bloated | Clean |
|---|---|
| utilize | use |
| methodology | method |
| facilitate | help |
| subsequently | then |
| in the event that | if |
| prior to | before |
| approximately | about |
| commence | start |

### Verb Intensity Ladder

Match verb strength to data magnitude. Overstatement destroys credibility; understatement buries the story.

| Magnitude | Decline verbs | Growth verbs |
|---|---|---|
| ~0% (neutral) | held steady, held flat | held steady, plateaued |
| ~0% (after decline) | bottomed out, stabilized | — |
| ~0% (after growth) | stalled, lost momentum | — |
| < 1% | dipped, edged down, softened | edged up, ticked up, inched higher |
| 1–5% | fell, declined, dropped | rose, grew, climbed |
| 5–15% | sank, slid, contracted | jumped, expanded, accelerated |
| 15–30% | tumbled, slumped, cratered | surged, soared, spiked |
| > 30% | collapsed, plummeted | skyrocketed, doubled/tripled |

**Direction-change verbs** (not magnitude-dependent):

| Pattern | Verbs |
|---|---|
| Reversal (up → down) | reversed, turned, pivoted |
| Reversal (down → up) | rebounded, recovered, bounced back |
| Acceleration | accelerated, gained momentum, steepened |
| Deceleration | slowed, lost momentum, eased, tapered |
| Volatility | fluctuated, whipsawed, oscillated |

**Mismatched:** "Revenue plummeted 3% last quarter." (3% is a decline, not a plummet)
**Matched:** "Revenue fell 3% last quarter — the third consecutive decline."

Use these thresholds as guardrails, not rigid rules. Context matters — a 3% GDP decline is catastrophic; a 3% dip in daily website traffic is noise.

### Kill Empty Intensifiers

Delete these words on sight: very, really, quite, extremely, significantly (unless reporting statistical significance), substantially, considerably, fairly, rather.

If the sentence weakens without the intensifier, the verb is too weak. Replace the verb.

**Weak:** "Revenue increased very significantly."
**Strong:** "Revenue surged 23%."

### Concrete Verbs Over Adjective+Be

| Adjective + be | Concrete verb |
|---|---|
| was very high | peaked, surged |
| was lower than | trailed, lagged |
| is different from | diverges from, departs from |
| was not enough | fell short of |
| is important | matters, drives, shapes |

## Tone — Authority Without Pomposity

### Three-Level Hedging

Target this proportion: **70% confident, 20% light hedge, 10% strong hedge.**

This ratio describes the *output* of good analytical writing, not a rule to force. If your evidence genuinely supports fewer confident claims, use the Probability Language Ladder (below) to match hedge strength to evidence. The 70/20/10 ratio is a smell test: if more than 30% of your claims are hedged, either the evidence is weak (acknowledge this) or you're over-hedging (tighten up).

| Level | Markers | When to use |
|---|---|---|
| Confident (no hedge) | "X is", "X shows", "the data confirm" | Strong evidence, clear patterns |
| Light hedge | "suggests", "indicates", "points to" | Reasonable inference, indirect evidence |
| Strong hedge | "may", "could", "appears to" | Speculative, limited data, early signals |

### Phrases to Delete on Sight

These add zero information. Cut every instance:

- "It's worth noting that" → just state the thing
- "It is important to note" → if it's important, the reader will know
- "Arguably" → either argue it or don't
- "One might say" → say it or don't
- "In terms of" → rephrase
- "At the end of the day" → delete
- "It goes without saying" → then don't say it
- "As a matter of fact" → delete
- "For all intents and purposes" → delete

### AI-Tell Phrases

These phrases signal AI-generated text. Readers who encounter them lose trust. Eliminate on sight:

- "Let's dive into..." / "Let's delve into..."
- "It's important to understand that..."
- "This is a multifaceted issue..."
- "There are several key factors..."
- "This underscores the importance of..."
- "Moving forward, it will be crucial to..."
- "In today's rapidly changing landscape..."
- "The data paints a compelling picture..."
- "This highlights the need for..."
- "A nuanced understanding of..."

Replace each with a direct statement of the point itself.

### Express Uncertainty Honestly

When data is limited, say so directly.

**Evasive:** "It could potentially be argued that, to some extent, there might be a relationship between these variables."
**Honest:** "The sample is too small to confirm a relationship. Three years of data suggest a correlation, but a longer time series would strengthen the case."

### Write for a Smart Non-Specialist

Write for a smart colleague outside your domain. Define field-specific terms on first use. Skip definitions for common concepts (percentage, trend, correlation).

**Over-explained:** "The Crime Severity Index (CSI), which is a composite measure that weights different types of crime by their relative severity as determined by sentencing patterns in Canadian courts, with more serious offences receiving higher weights, fell to 78.2."
**Right level:** "The Crime Severity Index — which weights crimes by their court-sentence severity — fell to 78.2, a two-decade low."

## Paragraphs — The Structural Unit

### One Idea Per Paragraph

If a paragraph covers two distinct points, split it. A paragraph earns its existence by advancing exactly one claim.

**Two ideas crammed in (split this):** "Crime fell 22% in BC over the past decade, driven primarily by property crime reductions in urban centres. Meanwhile, policing costs rose 34% over the same period, with the largest budget increases going to municipalities that already had the lowest crime rates."

**Split into two paragraphs:** First paragraph covers the crime decline (what fell, where, how much). Second paragraph covers the cost increase (the spending-outcome mismatch). Each gets its own evidence and implication.

### Length Guidelines

- **Analytical prose:** 3–5 sentences. Enough for claim + evidence + "so what."
- **Narrative/journalistic pacing:** 2–3 sentences. Faster rhythm, more white space.
- **Single-sentence paragraphs:** Use sparingly for emphasis. More than two per page dilutes the effect.

### Topic → Evidence → So What

Every paragraph follows this skeleton:

1. **Topic sentence:** States the paragraph's claim. A reader skimming only topic sentences should grasp the full argument.
2. **Evidence:** Data, examples, or comparisons that support the claim.
3. **So what:** The implication — what this means for the reader, the analysis, or the next question.

**Example:**
"Property crime fell 12% across BC in 2023, the steepest single-year decline since 2015. *[topic]* The drop concentrated in auto theft and break-and-enter, which together accounted for 80% of the reduction. *[evidence]* Rural detachments saw no improvement — suggesting urban-focused interventions drove the decline, not a province-wide shift. *[so what]*"

### Transitions: Old to New

Link the previous paragraph's conclusion to the next paragraph's opening. The reader should never wonder "why are we talking about this now?"

**End of paragraph 3:** "...the cost data reveals a gap between spending and outcomes."
**Start of paragraph 4:** "That gap widens when measured per capita."

#### Transition Toolkit

Use these connectors to link paragraphs. Pick the relationship, then choose a connector:

| Relationship | Connectors |
|---|---|
| Continuation | That pattern extends to...; The same dynamic plays out in...; Similarly... |
| Contrast | But...; Yet...; The opposite held for...; Not so in... |
| Escalation | Worse still...; The gap widens when...; That understates the problem... |
| Cause/effect | The result:...; That explains why...; The consequence was... |
| Time shift | By 2020...; Three years later...; Since then... |
| Scope shift | At the national level...; Within BC specifically...; Zooming in... |
| Concession | Granted...; This overstates the case somewhat...; The caveat:... |

#### Sentence Opener Variety Bank

When three sentences in a row start the same way, swap one for an alternative structure:

| Opener type | Examples |
|---|---|
| Temporal | "By 2023...", "Since the policy change...", "Over the past decade..." |
| Prepositional | "Across all provinces...", "Within the Lower Mainland...", "At the municipal level..." |
| Inverted | "Harder to explain is...", "More striking than the decline itself is..." |
| Comparative | "Unlike Ontario...", "Compared with the national average...", "Where Alberta diverged..." |
| Participial | "Adjusting for inflation...", "Measured per capita...", "Excluding outliers..." |
| Numeric lead | "Three factors drove...", "Of the ten provinces, seven..." |

### Paragraph Templates

Use these skeletons when drafting analytical paragraphs. Replace bracketed placeholders with real content.

**Finding paragraph:**
"[Metric] [verb matching magnitude] [number] in [time period] — [comparison that creates scale]. [One sentence of supporting evidence or breakdown]. [Implication: what this means or what question it raises]."

> Policing costs rose 8% in 2023 — the largest single-year jump since the 2010 municipal restructuring. Salary increases accounted for two-thirds of the rise, with overtime driving the rest. At this pace, policing will consume 40% of municipal budgets within five years.

**Contrast paragraph:**
"[Surface-level pattern]. But [exception or complication]. [Evidence for the exception]. [Revised understanding]."

> Property crime fell across every major city in 2023. But the drop was not uniform — Vancouver's rate fell 18%, while Surrey's barely moved. Auto theft drove Vancouver's improvement; break-and-enter, Surrey's dominant category, held steady. The "province-wide decline" is really a Vancouver story.

**Trend paragraph:**
"[Metric] has [trajectory verb] since [start year], [quantified change]. [Inflection point or acceleration]. [Forward-looking implication]."

> BC's Crime Severity Index has declined since 2003, falling 38% over two decades. The pace slowed after 2018, dropping just 4% in the final five years. If the deceleration continues, the index will flatline by 2027.

## Writing About Uncertainty and Probability

### Distinguish Data Limitations from Analytical Uncertainty

Data gaps and analytical uncertainty are different. Name which one applies.

- **Data limitation:** "Municipal-level data is unavailable before 2010, so this comparison starts there."
- **Analytical uncertainty:** "The correlation between funding and crime reduction is strong (r = 0.78), but the sample covers only twelve municipalities."

### Quantify When Possible

Vague uncertainty language ("some", "many", "often") erodes trust. Attach numbers or ranges.

**Vague:** "Many provinces saw increases."
**Precise:** "Seven of ten provinces saw increases, ranging from 2% to 14%."

### Probability Language Ladder

| Confidence | Language |
|---|---|
| >95% | "is", "confirms", "demonstrates" |
| 80–95% | "strongly suggests", "indicates" |
| 60–80% | "suggests", "points to" |
| 40–60% | "may", "could", "the data are consistent with" |
| <40% | "preliminary", "too early to conclude", "warrants further study" |

### Causation vs. Correlation in Prose

Analytical prose must distinguish between what the data shows (correlation) and what caused it (causation). Sloppy language implies causation where none is established.

| Implies causation (use carefully) | Implies correlation (safer default) |
|---|---|
| caused, drove, produced, led to | coincided with, tracked alongside, moved with |
| because of, as a result of | during the same period, alongside |
| increased crime | increased alongside rising crime |

**Causal claim:** "The new policing model reduced property crime by 15%." Use only if a controlled study or natural experiment supports the link.
**Correlation claim:** "Property crime fell 15% in the two years after the new policing model launched." States the facts without asserting mechanism.
**Hedged causal:** "The timing suggests the policing model contributed to the 15% decline, though other factors — including economic recovery — likely played a role."

Default to correlation language. Upgrade to causal language only when the evidence warrants it.

### Acknowledge Limits Without Apologizing

State limitations as facts, not confessions. One sentence, then move on.

**Apologetic:** "Unfortunately, due to the limitations of the available data, we were unable to fully account for all possible confounding variables, which means our findings should be interpreted with caution."
**Direct:** "The model does not control for immigration flows, which may influence the trend. With that caveat, the pattern holds across all population-adjusted scenarios."

## Data-Specific Writing

### Embed the "So What" in the Number

A statistic without context is trivia. Attach meaning to every number.

**Trivia:** "Unemployment rose to 4.5%."
**Contextual:** "Unemployment hit 4.5% — its highest level since 2015."
**Full context:** "Unemployment hit 4.5%, its highest since 2015, erasing three years of labour market gains."

### Create Scale with Comparisons

Raw numbers need reference points. Translate abstract figures into concrete terms.

- **Temporal:** "the largest increase since 2008"
- **Relative:** "twice the national average"
- **Tangible:** "$50M — enough to fund three regional hospitals for a year"
- **Per-unit:** "$1,200 per household"

### Three Forms for Every Key Statistic

Present important numbers in three ways:

1. **Percentage** for relative change: "grew 18%"
2. **Absolute** for scale: "added 45,000 jobs"
3. **Comparison** for context: "more than Saskatchewan and Manitoba combined"

You don't need all three every time, but every key statistic deserves at least two.

### Writing Comparisons

Comparisons drive analytical prose. Structure them to make the contrast unmistakable.

**Side-by-side:** "Vancouver's rate fell 18%; Surrey's held at 3%." Place the larger/more surprising value first.

**Ratio:** "Alberta grew twice as fast as the national average." Use ratios when the absolute numbers are less intuitive than the relationship.

**Gap framing:** "The gap between urban and rural crime rates widened from 15 points in 2015 to 28 points in 2024." Focus on the gap itself when the trend of divergence matters more than either value.

**Ranking:** "BC ranked third among provinces, behind only Ontario and Quebec." Use rankings when the reader's question is "where does X stand?"

**Avoid:** Listing parallel statistics without connecting them. "Vancouver's rate was 52. Surrey's rate was 48. Kelowna's rate was 61." Instead: "Kelowna led at 61, followed by Vancouver (52) and Surrey (48) — a tighter range than the provincial data suggests."

### Describing Charts

When writing narrative to accompany a chart:

1. **State the headline finding** — what the chart proves, not what it shows mechanically.
2. **Call out the exception** — the data point that breaks the pattern matters more than the pattern itself.
3. **Give the reader a reason to look** — frame the chart as evidence for a claim.

**Mechanical:** "Figure 3 shows crime rates from 2010 to 2024. The x-axis represents years and the y-axis represents incidents per 100,000."
**Analytical:** "Crime rates fell steadily from 2010 to 2019, then reversed course (Figure 3). The pandemic spike in 2020 gets the attention, but the real story is 2022–2024: rates climbed even after pandemic effects faded."

### Chart Type–Specific Narration

| Chart type | Lead with | Call out |
|---|---|---|
| Line/time series | The trajectory — "rose steadily", "reversed in 2020" | Inflection points, divergences between series |
| Bar comparison | The winner/loser — "Vancouver led at 42%" | Surprising gaps or near-ties |
| Heatmap | The dominant pattern — "northern regions consistently higher" | Cells that break the pattern |
| Scatter/correlation | The relationship — "strong positive correlation (r=0.82)" | Outliers that defy the trend |
| Stacked area | The composition shift — "services overtook goods in 2018" | Fastest-growing or shrinking segment |

### Writing About Geographic Patterns

Geographic analysis requires precise spatial language. Avoid vague "some regions" framing.

**Name the geography explicitly:**
- Weak: "Some regions performed better than others."
- Strong: "The Lower Mainland and Capital Region drove BC's improvement; the North Coast and Peace River lagged."

**Use geographic hierarchy for zoom:**
- National → Provincial → Regional → Municipal. State which level you're analyzing.
- "At the provincial level, BC ranked third. Within BC, the picture fractures: Vancouver improved 18%, while Prince George worsened 7%."

**Distinguish urban/rural carefully:**
- Define your threshold: "Urban centres (population >100,000)" vs. "rural detachments (population <10,000)."
- Avoid implying causation from geography alone: "Rural areas had higher crime" does not mean rurality causes crime.

**Map narrative to spatial pattern:**
- Cluster: "concentrated in", "clustered around", "centred on"
- Gradient: "decreasing from north to south", "radiating outward from"
- Exception: "the outlier was", "the only municipality that bucked the trend"

### Writing About Trends

Trends are the backbone of analytical writing. Describe them precisely.

**Name the trajectory and quantify it:**
- "Crime fell steadily" — how much? Over how long?
- Better: "Crime fell 22% over ten years, averaging about 2.5 points per year."

**Identify inflection points — they carry the story:**
- "The decline stalled in 2018" is more newsworthy than "the decline continued."
- Always quantify: "The decline stalled in 2018 — the rate dropped just 1.2% over the next three years, compared with 4% annually in the prior five."

**Distinguish trend from noise:**
- A single year's jump is not a reversal. Use "spike", "one-year uptick", or "anomaly" for single data points.
- Use "reversal", "shift", or "new trajectory" only for multi-year directional changes.
- "Crime rose in 2020" is a data point. "Crime has risen for three consecutive years" is a trend.

**Decompose compound trends:**
- "Crime fell overall" hides composition shifts. Break it down: "Property crime drove the decline; violent crime held steady."
- Aggregate numbers mislead when subcategories move in opposite directions. Always check.

### Writing Conclusions

Conclusions synthesize — they do not summarize. The reader already read the analysis. Give them what they haven't seen yet.

**Summary conclusion (weak):** "This report examined crime trends in BC from 2014 to 2024. We found that crime fell overall but rose in rural areas. Policing costs increased across all regions."

**Synthesis conclusion (strong):** "BC's crime decline is real but misleading — it reflects urban gains that mask rural stagnation. Policing budgets grew everywhere, but the largest increases went to jurisdictions where crime was already falling. The data points to a reallocation problem, not a spending problem."

**Three things a conclusion can do (pick one or two, not all):**
1. **Reframe:** Present the data from a new angle that changes interpretation.
2. **Recommend:** State what the findings imply for decisions or policy.
3. **Open:** Name the question the analysis cannot answer — what comes next.

Never repeat the nut graf. If the conclusion could replace the introduction, it adds nothing.

### Chart Captions and Annotations

Chart captions are not titles — they add information the chart cannot convey alone.

**Caption formula:** "[What the chart shows] + [time period] + [data source or note]"
> Property crime rate per 100,000 population, 2010–2024. Excludes traffic offences. Source: Statistics Canada, Table 35-10-0063-01.

**Annotation formula:** "[What happened] + [when] + [brief cause if known]"
> "Policy change" (with arrow pointing to 2018 inflection) is weaker than "New diversion program launched — intake shifted 2,000 cases from courts to community" (2018, with arrow).

**Source attribution:** Cite the source once per chart — either in the caption or in the narrative, not both. Duplicating "Source: Statistics Canada" across the chart footer and the prose wastes the reader's time.

Annotations should earn their space. If the data point is self-evident from the chart, skip the annotation. Annotate inflection points, outliers, and policy changes — the "why" behind a visible shift.

### Executive Summary Pattern

For reports or multi-section analyses, open with a 3–5 sentence executive summary:

1. **The headline:** One sentence capturing the most important finding.
2. **The scale:** Quantify the finding with the strongest available number.
3. **The surprise:** What the data reveals that contradicts assumptions or conventional wisdom.
4. **The implication:** What should change because of this finding.

> BC's crime rate fell 22% over the past decade, but the decline masks a widening urban-rural gap. Cities drove the improvement — rural communities saw crime hold steady or rise. Policing budgets, meanwhile, grew fastest in the jurisdictions that needed them least. Reallocating resources toward rural detachments would address the disparity more effectively than across-the-board increases.

### Writing for Different Formats

The same finding reads differently in a report, a chart annotation, and a slide bullet. Adjust density, not quality.

| Format | Length | Style | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Report paragraph | 3–5 sentences | Full claim→evidence→so-what | "Property crime fell 22%..." (see paragraph templates) |
| Executive summary bullet | 1–2 sentences | Finding + scale | "Property crime fell 22% — the steepest decade-long decline on record." |
| Chart annotation | 5–15 words | Label + number | "Steepest annual decline since 2015 (−8.3%)" |
| Slide bullet | 8–15 words | Headline-style, no verbs optional | "22% property crime decline — steepest in a decade" |
| Table footnote | 1 sentence | Methodological caveat | "Excludes traffic offences. Population-adjusted." |

All formats follow the same core rules: point-first, precise verbs, no filler. Only the density changes.

### Section Headers That Inform

Headers should preview the finding, not label the topic. A reader scanning only headers should grasp the argument.

| Label header (weak) | Finding header (strong) |
|---|---|
| Crime Trends | Crime Fell 22% — But Only in Cities |
| Budget Analysis | Budgets Grew Fastest Where Crime Fell Most |
| Geographic Distribution | The North Got Worse While the South Improved |
| Methodology | How We Measured: CSI, Population-Adjusted, 1998–2024 |

Reserve label headers ("Data Sources", "Appendix") for reference sections only.

### Number Presentation

**Round for readability.** Precision belongs in charts and tables, not prose.

| Context | Precision | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Headline finding | Round to nearest whole | "grew 22%" not "grew 21.7%" |
| Comparison of two values | Match precision | "rose 8% vs. 3%" not "rose 8.2% vs. 3%" |
| Dollar amounts > $1M | Round to nearest $0.1M or $1M | "$4.2 million" not "$4,187,342" |
| Dollar amounts < $1M | Round to nearest $1K or $10K | "$340,000" or "about $340K" |
| Population | Round to nearest 1K or 10K | "45,000 residents" not "44,823 residents" |
| Precise scientific result | Keep precision | "p = 0.032" stays as-is |

Write large numbers as words-plus-digits: "$4.2 billion", "1.4 million people." Avoid all-digit formats in prose ("$4,200,000,000" forces the reader to count commas).

**Percentage vs. percentage points:** These are different. If unemployment moves from 4% to 6%, that's a 2 *percentage point* increase but a 50% *relative* increase. Use "percentage points" for absolute differences between rates; use "percent" for relative change. Getting this wrong overstates or understates magnitude.

### Writing Key Findings

Key findings lists appear at the top of reports, in executive summaries, and as chart annotations. Each bullet must stand alone — a reader should understand the finding without context.

**Weak findings list:**
- Crime decreased
- There were regional differences
- Costs went up
- The data shows some interesting patterns

**Strong findings list:**
- Property crime fell 22% across BC from 2014 to 2024 — the steepest decade-long decline on record.
- Rural detachments bucked the trend: crime held steady or rose in 60% of communities north of Prince George.
- Policing costs grew 34% over the same period, with the largest budget increases concentrated in low-crime urban centres.
- The Crime Severity Index obscures a shift in composition: violent crime now accounts for a larger share of a smaller total.

Each finding follows the pattern: **[What happened] + [how much] + [why it matters or what's surprising].**

### Writing Method Sections

Method sections exist to build trust, not to show work. Keep them short and scannable.

**Pattern:**
1. **One sentence on scope:** What the analysis covers (geography, time period, unit of measurement).
2. **One sentence on source:** Where the data came from and how current it is.
3. **One sentence on method:** What transformations or calculations were applied.
4. **One sentence on limitations:** What the data cannot tell you.

**Bloated method section:**
"This analysis utilizes data obtained from Statistics Canada's Table 35-10-0063-01, which contains Crime Severity Index values for Canadian provinces and territories spanning the years 1998 through 2024. The data was downloaded on March 1, 2026 and subsequently cleaned by removing suppressed values, normalizing geographic names, and converting all numeric fields from string to float representations. Year-over-year percentage changes were then calculated using the standard formula."

**Tight method section:**
"This analysis covers provincial crime severity from 1998 to 2024 using Statistics Canada's Crime Severity Index (Table 35-10-0063-01, downloaded March 2026). Values are population-weighted and indexed to a 2006 baseline by StatCan's methodology. The CSI does not capture unreported crime, which surveys estimate at roughly 70% of incidents."

## Anti-Patterns — What to Eliminate

### Passive Voice Overuse

AI-generated text defaults to ~28% passive. Publication-quality prose stays under 10%. Scan every paragraph; if more than one sentence per five is passive, rewrite.

### Filler Phrases

| Filler | Replacement |
|---|---|
| in order to | to |
| at this point in time | now |
| during the course of | during |
| in the process of | while |
| on the basis of | based on |
| in light of the fact that | because |
| with respect to | about |
| in a manner that | so that |
| it should be noted that | *(delete)* |
| the fact that | *(rephrase)* |

### Repetitive Sentence Openers

If three consecutive sentences start with the same structure (subject-verb, "The data...", "This..."), rewrite at least one. Vary openers: lead with a prepositional phrase, a temporal marker, a comparison, or an inverted structure.

**Repetitive:** "The crime rate fell in 2022. The decline continued in 2023. The trend reversed in 2024."
**Varied:** "The crime rate fell in 2022. That decline continued the following year. By 2024, the trend had reversed."

### Over-Hedging

One hedge per claim. Stacking "might", "possibly", "perhaps", "to some extent" in the same sentence signals the writer doesn't trust the claim. Either strengthen the evidence or cut the claim.

**Over-hedged:** "This might possibly suggest that there could potentially be a relationship."
**Calibrated:** "The data suggest a relationship, though the sample is small."

### Data Dumps

Listing numbers without interpretation is the most common failure in analytical writing. Every number needs a verb and a "so what."

**Data dump:** "In 2020, the rate was 85.3. In 2021, it was 82.1. In 2022, it was 79.4. In 2023, it was 78.2."
**Analysis:** "The crime rate fell steadily from 85.3 in 2020 to 78.2 in 2023 — a cumulative 8% decline that brought BC to its lowest level in two decades."

If you catch yourself listing three or more numbers in consecutive sentences, stop. The reader needs the pattern, not the data.

### Trailing Summaries

Never end a section with "In summary, we have seen that..." or "To conclude, the above analysis shows..." The reader just read the section. Move forward, don't look back.

## Self-Check Checklist

Run this against every paragraph before finalizing:

- [ ] **Point-first?** Does the first sentence state the claim, not the setup?
- [ ] **Active voice?** Is the subject doing the action in most sentences?
- [ ] **No filler?** Are "in order to", "it should be noted", and similar phrases gone?
- [ ] **Verb matches magnitude?** Does "surged" actually describe >15% growth?
- [ ] **So what?** Does the paragraph explain why the data matters, not just what it shows?
- [ ] **One idea?** Does this paragraph advance exactly one claim?
- [ ] **Forward momentum?** Does the last sentence open a question rather than summarize?
- [ ] **Hedging calibrated?** Is there at most one hedge per claim?
- [ ] **Numbers in context?** Does every key statistic have a comparison or reference point?
- [ ] **No trailing summary?** Does the section end by advancing, not restating?
- [ ] **Causation honest?** Does the prose distinguish correlation from causation?
- [ ] **No AI-tells?** Are "dive into", "multifaceted", "crucial", "compelling" eliminated?
- [ ] **Trend vs. noise?** Are single-year changes described as data points, not trends?
- [ ] **Comparisons structured?** Are parallel statistics connected, not just listed?
- [ ] **No data dumps?** Are three+ consecutive numbers synthesized into a pattern instead?
- [ ] **% vs. pp correct?** Are rate differences described as "percentage points", not "percent"?

## Full Rewrite Example

This before/after demonstrates multiple rules applied to a single paragraph.

**Before (typical AI-generated analytical prose):**
"It is worth noting that, during the course of our analysis, we found that the crime rate in British Columbia experienced a significant decline of approximately 22% over the ten-year period from 2014 to 2024. This decline was primarily driven by reductions in property crime categories, particularly auto theft and break-and-enter offences. However, it should be noted that this overall trend masks important regional variations, as rural communities did not experience the same level of improvement as their urban counterparts."

**Issues identified:**
- "It is worth noting that" — filler (delete)
- "during the course of" — filler (replace with "from 2014 to 2024")
- "experienced a significant decline of approximately 22%" — nominalization + intensifier + buried number
- "This decline was primarily driven by" — passive voice
- "it should be noted that" — filler (delete)
- "important regional variations" — vague intensifier
- "did not experience the same level of improvement as their urban counterparts" — wordy passive

**After (applying this skill's rules):**
"BC's crime rate fell 22% from 2014 to 2024 — the steepest decade-long drop on record. Property crime drove the decline: auto theft and break-and-enter offences together accounted for 80% of the reduction. But the improvement stopped at city limits. Rural communities saw no decline, and in some northern detachments, crime rose."

**What changed:**
- Point-first: the finding leads
- "Fell 22%" replaces "experienced a significant decline of approximately 22%"
- Active voice throughout
- All filler deleted
- "So what" embedded: "steepest decade-long drop on record"
- Forward momentum: "crime rose" opens the question of why

## Revision Protocol

When revising a draft, run these passes in order. Each pass targets one dimension — do not try to fix everything at once.

1. **Structure pass:** Is every paragraph point-first? Does the piece open with a nut graf? Cut any paragraph that doesn't advance a claim.
2. **Voice pass:** Highlight every passive construction. Convert to active unless the actor is unknown. Target <10%.
3. **Filler pass:** Search-and-destroy: "in order to", "it should be noted", "the fact that", and all phrases from the filler table. Delete or replace each.
4. **Verb pass:** Check every verb against the intensity ladder. Flag mismatches (e.g., "plummeted" for a 3% drop).
5. **Numbers pass:** Does every statistic have context? Add comparisons, temporal anchors, or per-unit breakdowns where missing.
6. **Hedge pass:** Count hedges per paragraph. More than one per claim? Strengthen or cut.
7. **Rhythm pass:** Read aloud (or mentally). Flag three consecutive sentences of similar length. Vary with short punches or longer compounds.
8. **AI-tell pass:** Scan for "dive into", "multifaceted", "crucial", "compelling", "highlights the need". Replace with direct statements.
